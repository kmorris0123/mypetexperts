Yes, cats can eat a little plain cilantro. The ASPCA lists cilantro as non-toxic to cats. But cats are meat eaters and
get nothing they need from herbs. **A leaf or two is fine, but it is not a food your cat needs.**

## Is cilantro safe for cats?

The ASPCA lists cilantro as non-toxic to dogs, cats, and horses. The plant goes by other names too, including coriander
and Chinese parsley. All of these are the same plant, *Coriandrum sativum*. The ASPCA page lists no signs of poisoning
for it.

Non-toxic does not mean "no effects at all." The ASPCA says that eating any plant material may cause vomiting and an
upset stomach in cats and dogs. A cat that eats a lot of cilantro at once could throw up or get loose stool. If that
happens, don't offer more, and call your vet if your cat seems sick or the signs don't pass.

## Do cats need cilantro?

No. Cornell's Feline Health Center says cats are obligate carnivores. That means they rely on nutrients found only in
animal products. Cilantro adds a bit of taste and scent, but it gives your cat little that its regular food doesn't
already provide.

The Merck Veterinary Manual says to feed cats a complete and balanced food made for cats, and to avoid table scraps.
Think of cilantro as a small taste, not as nutrition.

## How can I serve cilantro to my cat safely?

If your cat is curious, keep it simple:

- Offer one small, plain leaf. Wash it first to rinse off dirt.
- Skip anything with salt, oil, sauce, or other seasoning.
- Watch your cat for the next day. If you see vomiting or diarrhea, stop.
- Give it only once in a while. Your cat's regular food should stay the main meal.

Cornell says treats should stay at or below about 10 to 15 percent of a cat's daily calories. A leaf of cilantro is
nowhere near that, but it is a good reminder that extras should stay small.

## Which dishes with cilantro should my cat avoid?

The herb is not the problem. The other ingredients can be.

- **Salsa and guacamole.** These often contain onion or garlic. The ASPCA says onion, garlic, and chives can irritate
  the gut and damage red blood cells, which can lead to anemia (too few healthy red blood cells). It adds that cats are
  more at risk than dogs. See our guides on [onions](/can-cats-eat-onions/) and
  [garlic](/can-cats-eat-garlic/) for cats.
- **Curries, soups, and stir-fries.** Cooked dishes often have salt, spices, and onion or garlic as well.
- **Anything creamy.** The ASPCA says pets don't make much of the enzyme that digests lactose, so dairy can cause
  diarrhea or an upset stomach. Cream sauces and dips with cilantro are best kept away from your cat.

If your cat ate a dish with onion or garlic in it, call your vet.

## Can cats eat cilantro leaves, stems, and seeds?

The ASPCA entry covers the plant as a whole, and it lists no problem parts. Even so, the sources we read say nothing
about how stems or seeds (coriander seeds) affect cats. Plain leaves are the simplest choice. Skip spice blends, which
may hold other ingredients.

## What if my cat keeps chewing my herb plants?

Some cats just like to chew on green things. If your cat goes after the cilantro on your windowsill, you don't need to
panic, since the plant is non-toxic. But check what else is growing nearby. The ASPCA keeps a searchable list of
[toxic and non-toxic plants](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants) you can
use before you bring any new plant home. Keep any plant on that list's toxic side out of your cat's reach.

## What are better treats for cats?

The best treat is one made for cats, in a small amount. Merck says to avoid table scraps. For another herb, see our
guide on [whether cats can eat basil](/can-cats-eat-basil/).
