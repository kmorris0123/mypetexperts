Yes, cats can eat a little tuna, but only as an **occasional treat**. Tuna made for people isn't a balanced diet for
cats, and cats that eat a lot of it over time can run into real health problems.

<Callout type="info" title="The short version">
  - A few small bites of plain canned tuna once in a while is fine for most healthy cats.
  - Pick tuna packed in water with no added salt, and drain it.
  - Don't feed it every day or use it in place of cat food.
  - Tuna-flavored cat food is a different product and is fine to feed as directed.
</Callout>

## Why tuna shouldn't be a regular meal

Cats love tuna, which is exactly the problem. Some cats will happily eat it in place of their real food. But a can of
tuna is just fish. It isn't formulated to cover everything a cat needs.

The Merck Veterinary Manual notes that diets built from just a few foods are often inadequate for cats. It also
describes a specific tuna problem: cats fed large amounts of canned tuna have developed vitamin E deficiency, which can
lead to a painful condition called pansteatitis, or "yellow fat disease," where the body's fat becomes inflamed. Signs
include poor appetite, listlessness, and tenderness when the belly is touched.

Cornell's Feline Health Center adds that some cats that ate canned fish meant for people developed potentially serious
neurological problems.

None of this means one spoonful will hurt your cat. It means tuna belongs in the treat category, not the food bowl.

## Mercury

Fish near the top of the food chain build up mercury from what they eat, and tuna carries more than many other fish.
According to Merck, fish-based products have been linked to long-term mercury poisoning in cats. Mercury mainly affects the
nervous system and kidneys, and it builds up over months or years of regular exposure rather than from a single snack.

The type of tuna matters. In FDA testing, canned albacore (white) tuna averaged about 0.35 parts per million of mercury,
roughly three times the 0.13 ppm average for canned light tuna. Neither is a reason to panic over an occasional bite,
but it's one more reason to keep tuna small and infrequent.

## Raw tuna and thiamine

Some raw fish contains enzymes called thiaminases, which break down thiamine (vitamin B1). Merck explains that cooking
destroys these enzymes, and the heat used in canning is enough to do it. So canned tuna isn't the thiamine concern.

A thiamine deficiency in cats can cause poor appetite, weakness, nerve problems, and seizures. Merck lists raw
freshwater fish and clams as the main sources of thiaminase, but raw fish in general also carries the same germ risks
vets raise about raw meat, which Cornell doesn't recommend for cats as food or as a treat. **Skip sushi and sashimi
for your cat.**

## How to serve tuna safely

- **Choose tuna packed in water.** Oil adds a lot of extra fat and calories.
- **Look for no salt added,** and avoid tuna in brine. The ASPCA warns that too much salt can cause thirst, vomiting,
  diarrhea, and in serious cases tremors or seizures.
- **Drain it well** and offer just a few small flakes.
- **Keep it plain.** No mayo, no tuna salad, and nothing seasoned. Tuna salad and tuna casseroles often include onion
  or garlic, which can damage a cat's red blood cells. The ASPCA notes cats are more sensitive to these than dogs.
- **Keep it occasional,** not a daily habit.

If your cat ate tuna mixed with onion or garlic, call your vet or a poison hotline for advice: ASPCA Animal Poison
Control at [(888) 426-4435](tel:+18884264435) or Pet Poison Helpline at [(855) 764-7661](tel:+18557647661). Both may
charge a fee.

## Cat-food tuna is different

Tuna-flavored cat food and tuna you'd buy for a sandwich are not the same thing. Cornell explains that commercial cat
foods are developed to give cats the right balance of nutrients and calories, and International Cat Care recommends
looking for food labeled "complete," which means it's made to meet all of a cat's nutritional needs.

So a tuna-flavored food labeled complete for your cat's life stage is fine to feed as a regular meal. Tuna cat
*treats* and toppers are a different story. Like most treats, they usually aren't complete, so check the label.

## How much is too much?

Veterinary sources don't give a specific tuna limit, so follow the general rule for treats. Cornell says treats
shouldn't make up more than 10 to 15 percent of your cat's daily calories. For tuna, that means a few small flakes now
and then, not a bowlful. If your cat has a health condition or is on a prescription diet, ask your vet before offering
any.
