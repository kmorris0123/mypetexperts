No. Dogs should not eat cooked bones of any kind. Cooking makes bones dry and brittle, so they break into sharp
pieces. These can cause choking and can cut or poke the mouth, throat, and gut. The American Kennel Club (AKC) says to
never give your dog a cooked bone.

## Why are cooked bones bad for dogs?

The AKC says cooked bones splinter into shards. Those shards can cause several problems:

- **Choking.** A piece can get stuck in the throat.
- **Cuts and holes.** Sharp pieces can hurt the mouth, tongue, throat, stomach, and intestines.
- **Blockage.** A large piece can get stuck in the gut. Surgery may be needed to remove it.
- **Broken teeth.** Hard, brittle bones can crack teeth.

The AKC also notes that cooking takes some nutrients out of bones, so there is little to gain.

## What are the signs of trouble?

The Merck Veterinary Manual says bones are the most common thing to get stuck in a dog's esophagus. That is the tube from the mouth to the stomach. Signs of a stuck bone include:

- Sudden, heavy drooling
- Gagging
- Bringing food or water back up
- Trying to swallow again and again

If it is not treated, dogs may stop eating, lose weight, and seem very tired. Merck says a stuck object should be removed right away. Vets often do this with a thin camera tube and small tongs through the mouth. The AKC also tells owners to watch for signs of a blockage, such as trouble pooping, and for choking.

## What should I do if my dog ate a cooked bone?

1. Take the rest of the bone away.
2. Call your vet and say what kind of bone it was and how much your dog ate.
3. Follow your vet's advice. They may want to see your dog to rule out serious harm.
4. Go to an emergency clinic right away if your dog chokes, gags, drools a lot, or seems to be in pain.

Don't try to pull a bone out of your dog's throat or make your dog vomit unless a vet tells you to.

## Other kinds of cooked bones

The AKC's rule is simple: no cooked bones of **any** kind. The sections below cover bones people often ask about.

### Are cooked pork bones okay for dogs?

No. Pork bones follow the same rule as other cooked bones. The AKC adds that pork rib bones are high in fat. That much fat can cause pancreatitis. Signs include vomiting, tiredness, diarrhea, and not wanting to eat. In bad cases it can be deadly. Sauces and spices on cooked ribs can also be a problem. The AKC notes that seasonings like salt, garlic, and onion can harm or poison dogs.

### Can dogs eat ham bones or ham hock bones?

No. A ham bone or ham hock bone is a cooked pork bone, so the same warning applies. A big ham bone left after a holiday meal is still a cooked bone. Don't hand it to your dog, and keep it out of an open trash can.

### What about pig bones and cooked pork shoulder bones?

No. Pork shoulder bones, pig bones, and pork rib bones all count as cooked pork bones when they come off the grill, oven, or smoker. The AKC says to avoid them all.

### Are knuckle bones safe for dogs?

Not when they are cooked. A cooked knuckle bone is still a cooked bone, and the AKC says no cooked bones. The AKC also says even raw bones that are too hard can crack teeth. Experts disagree about raw bones. If you ever give one, the AKC advises these steps:

- Choose a bone that fits your dog's size.
- Watch your dog the whole time.
- Take it away after 10 to 15 minutes.
- Throw it out after 3 to 4 days.
- Keep other dogs away.

### Can dogs eat cooked rabbit bones?

No. The sources we read do not single out rabbit, but the AKC's rule covers cooked bones from any animal. Rabbit bones are small, and we found no veterinary source that says cooked ones are safe. When in doubt, don't share them.

### Are slow-cooked bones softer and safer?

We found no veterinary source that says slow cooking makes bones safe. The AKC says to never give cooked bones of any kind, and that includes slow-cooked ones. A bone that looks soft may still break into sharp pieces, so skip it.

## What can my dog chew instead?

The AKC suggests commercially made chew toys and fake bones as safer options. Pick one that fits your dog's size and watch your dog while it chews. Any treat, including chews, should be no more than about 10% of your dog's daily calories.
