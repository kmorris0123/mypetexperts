Better not. Cooked knuckle bones are not safe, because cooked bones are brittle and can splinter. Raw bones are a risk too. They can crack teeth, cause a blockage, or carry germs. Vets disagree about raw bones, so ask your own vet before you give one.

## Are knuckle bones safe for dogs?

We did not find a vet source that talks about knuckle bones by name. So we use the general rules for bones, which apply to a knuckle bone from a cow or a pig.

The AKC says cooked bones are more likely to splinter. Splinters can break teeth and can cut the throat or gut. That is why the AKC says not to give cooked bones. A knuckle bone from a roast, a stew, or a smoker counts as a cooked bone. For more, see [can dogs eat cooked bones?](/can-dogs-eat-cooked-bones/)

## What about raw knuckle bones?

The AKC says some vets suggest raw meaty bones, and that raw bones do not splinter like cooked ones. But the AKC lists clear risks:

- **Broken teeth.** If a bone is harder than a tooth, the tooth can crack. That may mean costly dental care.
- **Germs.** Raw bones can carry bacteria such as salmonella and E. coli. They can also spoil within a few days if your dog doesn't finish them.
- **Upset stomach.** Any bone can cause diarrhea or other stomach trouble.
- **Blockage.** Vets report that dogs sometimes need surgery to remove a blockage caused by a bone.

The FDA also warns about raw pet food in general. In its tests, 15 of 196 raw dog and cat food samples had Salmonella, and 32 had Listeria. That study tested raw food, not bones. But the FDA says the germs can spread to people who touch the food, the bowl, or the surfaces around it.

## What are the signs of a bone problem?

The Merck Veterinary Manual says bones are the most common thing to get stuck in a dog's esophagus. That is the tube between the mouth and the stomach. Signs include:

- Sudden, heavy drooling
- Gagging
- Bringing food or water back up
- Trying to swallow again and again

If it isn't treated, a dog may stop eating, lose weight, and seem very tired. Merck says a stuck object should be removed right away. Vets often do this through the mouth with a thin camera tube and small tongs.

The AKC adds other warning signs. Vomiting, tiredness, diarrhea, and not wanting to eat can point to pancreatitis, a painful swelling of the pancreas. It can be deadly, so see a vet right away. Pain or trouble pooping can mean a piece of bone is stuck near the rectum.

## How can I lower the risk if I give a raw bone?

Talk to your vet first. If your vet agrees, the AKC gives these tips:

1. **Pick a big bone.** It should be longer than your dog's muzzle, so your dog can't swallow it whole.
2. **Skip bones cut into small pieces.** The AKC says these are a choking risk.
3. **Watch the whole time.** Take the bone away if it breaks apart or gets small.
4. **Teach "give."** It helps you take the bone away safely.
5. **Offer it after a meal,** and keep other dogs away.
6. **Take it away after 10 to 15 minutes.** Keep it in the fridge.
7. **Throw it out after 3 to 4 days.**

Wash your hands and anything the bone touched, using hot soapy water. The FDA advises this after you handle raw food.

## What should I do if my dog ate a knuckle bone?

Take away what is left. Call your vet and say whether the bone was cooked or raw, how big it was, and how much your dog ate. Follow your vet's advice. Go to an emergency clinic right away if you see choking, heavy drooling, repeated gagging, or signs of pain.

## What can my dog chew instead?

The AKC suggests chew toys and fake bones made for dogs. Choose one that fits your dog's size, and watch while your dog chews. Chews count as treats, and treats should be no more than about 10% of your dog's daily calories.
