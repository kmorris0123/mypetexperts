Yes, dogs can eat beef broth, but only a plain one. Pick broth with no onion, no garlic, and little or no added salt. Onion and garlic can harm a dog's red blood cells, and too much salt can make a dog sick. Check the label first.

<Callout type="danger" title="If your dog ate broth with onion, garlic, or lots of salt">
  Call your vet, an emergency clinic, or a poison hotline. This matters most if your dog ate a lot, or ate a strong product like bouillon cubes or powder.

  - ASPCA Animal Poison Control: [(888) 426-4435](tel:+18884264435)
  - Pet Poison Helpline: [(855) 764-7661](tel:+18557647661)

  Both may charge a consultation fee. Don't make your dog vomit at home unless a vet tells you to. Never use salt to make a dog vomit.
</Callout>

## Why does the label matter?

Plain cooked beef is a common food for dogs (see [can dogs eat beef](/can-dogs-eat-beef/)). Broth is beef flavor in a liquid, so any problem comes from what else is in the pot.

The AKC points out that some broths have onions, garlic, or salt added for taste and to keep the broth fresh longer. Those are the three things to look for on the ingredient list. A short, clear ingredient list is best.

## Is onion or garlic in beef broth dangerous for dogs?

It can be. Pet Poison Helpline says onions, garlic, chives, and leeks are all in the same plant family, and they are poisonous to dogs. A big enough dose damages red blood cells and can lead to anemia, which means the blood can't carry enough oxygen. It can also upset the stomach and cause vomiting and diarrhea.

Pet Poison Helpline also says Japanese breeds such as Akitas and Shiba Inus are more sensitive. The pages I read don't say how much onion or garlic is in a bowl of broth, or how much would hurt a dog. So the safest choice is broth with none. For more, see our guides on [onions](/can-dogs-eat-onions/) and [garlic](/can-dogs-eat-garlic/).

## Is salt in beef broth a problem?

It can be if your dog gets a lot. The ASPCA says too much salt can cause extra thirst and peeing. In worse cases it can cause vomiting, diarrhea, tremors, seizures, and even death. Pet Poison Helpline lists the same kinds of signs.

A small spoonful of broth is not likely to be that much. But many store-bought broths are salty, and it adds up. That is why plain, low-salt broth is the better pick. I did not find a source that gives a safe amount of salt for broth, so I can't give you a number. Keep fresh water out for your dog.

## How can I serve beef broth safely?

- Choose broth with a short ingredient list and no onion, garlic, or added salt. Or make plain broth at home.
- Strain out every bone and bit of meat before you serve it. The AKC says cooked bones are very dangerous for dogs. See [can dogs eat cooked bones](/can-dogs-eat-cooked-bones/).
- Let it cool first.
- Start with a small amount. Pour it over dry food, or serve a little on its own.
- Use it as an extra, not a full meal.

The AKC says broth is a way to get fluid and flavor to dogs with an upset stomach, and it can tempt dogs who are not eating well. The AKC gives no studies for the health benefits it lists, such as joint or skin support. Treat those as claims, not proof. A dog that is not eating, or keeps vomiting, needs a vet.

The AKC says shelf-stable broth generally needs to be used within seven days, which is too short for a small dog to finish a large amount. Check the storage directions on the carton.

## What about beef stock and bouillon?

**Beef stock** is a close cousin of broth. The same rules apply: no onion, garlic, or lots of salt. Read the label, because stock may have added flavorings.

**Bouillon cubes and powders** are made to be strong in flavor, and many have salt, onion, or garlic. Skip them. Use plain broth instead.

If you want to compare, our guide on [chicken broth for dogs](/can-dogs-eat-chicken-broth/) covers the same label checks.

## What should I do if my dog ate broth with onion or garlic?

Call your vet or a poison hotline now. Tell them what was in the broth, how much your dog ate, and how big your dog is. Pet Poison Helpline says the amount eaten and the size of the pet both affect how risky it is, so don't guess that a small amount is fine. Don't wait for signs to show up.

Watch for vomiting and diarrhea, and call a vet if your dog seems weak or tired in the days after.

## Other things to pour over food

Treats and extras, including broth, should stay small. A common guide is to keep treats to about 10% of your dog's daily calories. Broth also has little to offer as a full meal, so keep feeding your dog's regular food.
