Cats can have a small bite of **plain** rotisserie chicken meat once in a while. The skin, bones, and seasoning are the
problems. Many store-bought chickens are seasoned, and onion and garlic are especially risky for cats. If you can't
tell what is on it, don't share it.

<Callout type="warn" title="Before you share">
  - Pull off the skin and every bone first.
  - Offer only a few small bites of plain meat from the inside.
  - If the chicken has onion, garlic, or heavy seasoning, skip it.
  - Check the ingredient list or ask the store what was used.
</Callout>

## Why is rotisserie chicken a problem for cats?

Plain cooked chicken itself is not the issue. Cornell says cats rely on nutrients found only in animal foods. The trouble
comes from what is added to a rotisserie chicken, and from the parts you can't safely feed.

### Onion and garlic in the seasoning

The ASPCA says onions, garlic, and chives can irritate the gut and damage red blood cells, which can lead to anemia.
Anemia means the body has too few healthy red blood cells. The ASPCA also says cats are more susceptible than dogs.

The Merck Veterinary Manual calls cats the most susceptible species. It says powders and dried forms are the most common
cause of poisoning. Cooking does not make onion or garlic safe. Merck reports that cats have been poisoned by less than a
teaspoon of cooked onion.

I couldn't find a source that lists what any one brand puts on its chicken. That is why checking the label matters.

### Salt

The ASPCA says too much salt can make pets very thirsty and can cause vomiting, diarrhea, tremors, and seizures. A bite
of seasoned chicken is unlikely to reach that point, but salty food is one more reason to keep portions tiny.

### Bones

Cornell says eating poultry bones can block the digestive tract. The ASPCA says bones can injure or block a pet's gut,
and sometimes surgery is needed. For more, see our guide on [bones for cats](/can-cats-eat-bones/).

### Skin and fat

Cornell says fat from meat can make pancreatitis more likely in cats. The ASPCA says fatty foods can cause vomiting and
diarrhea, and may lead to pancreatitis. The skin and the juices at the bottom of the container are the fattiest parts.

## How do I share rotisserie chicken safely?

If the chicken is plain, you can offer a little.

1. Let it cool, so it is not hot.
2. Remove all skin, fat, and bones.
3. Tear a few bites of plain meat into small pieces.
4. Give it as a treat, not a meal.

Cornell says treats should be no more than 10 to 15 percent of a cat's daily calories, and should be given only once in a
while. A complete cat food should still be the main meal. For plain chicken in general, see our guide to
[chicken for cats](/can-cats-eat-chicken/).

## What are the signs my cat ate something harmful?

Merck says the signs of onion or garlic poisoning often don't show up right away. They can take a few days. They include:

- Tiredness and not wanting to eat
- Weakness
- Fast breathing
- Pale or yellow gums
- Dark urine

For a bone, or a lot of fatty skin, watch for vomiting, belly pain, or a cat that stops eating. The ASPCA names vomiting and
diarrhea as common results of fatty foods.

<Callout type="danger" title="If your cat ate seasoned chicken, skin, or bones">
  Call your vet, an emergency clinic, or a poison hotline. This matters most if your cat ate onion, garlic, or a bone, even
  if your cat seems fine. Signs of onion and garlic poisoning can take days to appear.

  - ASPCA Animal Poison Control: [(888) 426-4435](tel:+18884264435)
  - Pet Poison Helpline: [(855) 764-7661](tel:+18557647661)

  Both may charge a consultation fee. Don't try to make your cat vomit at home unless a vet tells you to.
</Callout>

## What can I give my cat instead?

Plain chicken you cook yourself is the easier choice. Boil or bake it with no salt, oil, onion, or garlic. Treats
made for cats are another simple option. See also our guides on
[garlic for cats](/can-cats-eat-garlic/) and [foods that are toxic to cats](/foods-toxic-to-cats/).
