No, wine gums are not a good treat for dogs. Regular ones are mostly sugar, and the AKC says dogs are healthier without
human sweets. **The bigger worry is sugar-free gummy candy.** It may contain xylitol, a sweetener that is very
dangerous to dogs.

<Callout type="danger" title="If your dog ate sugar-free candy or gummies">
  Call your vet, an emergency clinic, or a poison hotline **now**, even if your dog seems fine. The signs of xylitol
  poisoning can start fast, and some take a day to show.

  - ASPCA Animal Poison Control: [(888) 426-4435](tel:+18884264435)
  - Pet Poison Helpline: [(855) 764-7661](tel:+18557647661)

  Both may charge a consultation fee. Don't make your dog vomit at home unless a vet tells you to.
</Callout>

## Are wine gums toxic to dogs?

Plain wine gums are not a known poison. We did not find a veterinary source that names wine gums or gives a safe
amount, so we can't say how many are okay. The AKC says dogs should not have any sweets in their diet, so skipping
them is the best plan.

Sugar can still cause trouble. The ASPCA says large amounts of sugar can make a dog very thirsty. It can also cause
watery diarrhea and dehydration. In severe cases, it can lead to wobbliness, tremors, and seizures.

## Why is xylitol the real danger?

Xylitol is a sugar substitute. The Merck Veterinary Manual says it is found in sugar-free gum, candy, mints, peanut
butter, and baked goods. It also shows up in some vitamins, toothpaste, and medicines.

Dogs are the only common pet at risk, says Merck. In dogs, xylitol can do two things:

- **Drop blood sugar.** Merck says doses above about 100 mg per kg of body weight (45 mg per pound) have been linked to this.
- **Hurt the liver.** Merck says some dogs that eat much more than that get severe liver failure. This is uncommon but serious.

Merck says low blood sugar can start within 30 minutes. With some products it can be delayed 12 to 18 hours.

## How do I check candy for xylitol?

Read the ingredient list, even on candy marketed as sugar-free or low sugar. The FDA says xylitol may also be listed as
birch sugar or wood sugar. The AKC adds birch bark extract. Xylitol can show up in places you would not expect, so it
helps to check labels on other foods in your home too.

Not all sweeteners are as risky. The AKC says sweeteners like stevia, sucralose, and erythritol are less dangerous,
though large amounts can upset the stomach. Xylitol is the one to avoid.

## What signs should I watch for?

The FDA says vomiting often comes first. Then dogs may become weak, move less, stagger, or collapse. Seizures can follow.

Merck lists signs of liver injury too: tiredness, vomiting, and yellow skin or gums (jaundice). These can take 24 to 48
hours or longer to appear.

## What should I do if my dog ate wine gums?

1. **Check the package.** Look for xylitol, birch sugar, or wood sugar in the ingredients. Keep the package to show your vet.
2. **Call your vet or a poison hotline** if xylitol is on the label, or if you can't tell.
3. **Don't wait for signs.** The FDA says some serious effects may not show for 12 to 24 hours, and a dog may need
   to stay at the vet for monitoring. Merck says treatment can include checking blood sugar often and giving sugar through an IV.
4. **If it was regular candy,** and a small amount, watch for upset stomach. Call your vet if your dog ate a lot, or if
   you are not sure what was in it.

Merck says dogs with simple low blood sugar usually do well when treated quickly. Dogs with severe liver injury have a
more guarded outlook.

## What about other gummy candy and gummy vitamins?

Gummy bears and similar candy follow the same rules: no sweets as a treat, and check for xylitol. Gummy vitamins and
supplements can be riskier. The ASPCA says some contain xylitol, extra vitamin D, or iron. Others may contain THC, the part of marijuana that gets
people high. Keep all gummies in a cabinet your dog can't open.
