Chicken fried rice is not a good snack for dogs. The rice and chicken are fine on their own. The problem is what is cooked in with them. Takeout and homemade fried rice often has **onion, garlic, and a lot of salt**, and these can make dogs sick.

<Callout type="danger" title="If your dog ate a lot of fried rice, or you know it had onion or garlic">
  Call your vet, an emergency clinic, or a poison hotline for advice. Do this even if your dog seems fine, because some signs show up days later.

  - ASPCA Animal Poison Control: [(888) 426-4435](tel:+18884264435)
  - Pet Poison Helpline: [(855) 764-7661](tel:+18557647661)

  Both may charge a consultation fee. Don't make your dog vomit at home unless a vet tells you to.
</Callout>

## Why is chicken fried rice a problem for dogs?

Plain cooked rice is okay for dogs. The AKC says dogs can eat rice that has been boiled in water. It also warns against seasonings and spices, which it says can upset a dog's stomach or even be toxic.

Fried rice is the opposite of plain. Many recipes use onion, garlic, scallions, and salty sauce. I did not find a vet source that tests fried rice itself, so the risk comes from those ingredients, not from the dish as a whole. Recipes vary, and you often can't tell what is in a restaurant's version.

## Are onion and garlic in fried rice dangerous?

They can be. The ASPCA says onion, garlic, and chives can upset the stomach and damage red blood cells, which can lead to anemia. Anemia means the blood can't carry enough oxygen.

The Merck Veterinary Manual adds these facts:

- Cooking does not make onion or garlic safe. Dogs and cats have been poisoned by raw, cooked, dried, and powdered forms.
- Garlic is 3 to 5 times more toxic than onion.
- Dried and powdered forms, such as onion powder and dry soup mixes, are the ones most often linked to poisoning.
- Red blood cell damage happens slowly. It usually shows 3 to 5 days after eating.

The Manual says dogs showed signs after eating 15 to 30 grams of raw onion per kilogram of body weight. That is a large amount, and these numbers are for raw onion, not fried rice. The sources I read don't say how much onion is in a serving of fried rice. So a few grains with a little onion are unlikely to be a big problem, but a bowl is a different story.

## Is the salt in fried rice a problem?

It can be, if your dog eats a lot. The Merck Veterinary Manual says too much salt can make a dog vomit within a few hours. In worse cases it can lead to weakness, diarrhea, muscle tremors, and seizures. The ASPCA also lists extra thirst and peeing.

The Manual says signs can start when a dog eats 2 to 3 grams of salt per kilogram of body weight. A small dog that gets into a whole takeout box could be at more risk than a large dog that eats a bite. I did not find a source that lists the salt in fried rice or soy sauce. Check labels if you can.

## What are the signs to watch for?

Watch your dog for the next few days if it ate a real amount of fried rice. The sources list these signs:

- **Soon after eating (salt):** vomiting, extra thirst, more peeing, diarrhea
- **Serious salt signs:** weakness, muscle tremors, seizures
- **Days later (onion and garlic):** tiredness, weakness, not wanting to eat, fast breathing, yellow skin or gums, dark urine, collapse

Call your vet right away for tremors, seizures, collapse, or trouble breathing.

## What should I do if my dog ate fried rice?

1. **Find out what was in it.** Check the recipe or ask the restaurant about onion, garlic, and scallions.
2. **Take away the rest** and give your dog fresh water.
3. **Call your vet or a poison hotline** if your dog ate more than a bite, if the dish had onion or garlic, or if your dog is small.
4. **Watch closely** for several days, because red blood cell damage can show up late.

The Merck Veterinary Manual says a vet can treat a recent exposure by emptying the stomach, giving fluids, and giving oxygen or a blood transfusion in severe cases. Don't try to treat it at home.

## What can I feed instead?

A small amount of **plain rice cooked in water** is a much better choice. The AKC says to skip the seasoning and watch portions, because too many carbohydrates can cause weight gain. Start slowly with any new food, and ask your vet first if your dog has a health problem.

Plain, cooked chicken with no skin, bones, or seasoning is another simple treat. See our guide on [whether dogs can eat chicken](/can-dogs-eat-chicken/), and read more about [plain rice for dogs](/can-dogs-eat-rice/).

Keep treats to about 10% of your dog's daily calories. For the full list of foods to avoid, see [foods that are toxic to dogs](/foods-toxic-to-dogs/). You can also read about [onions](/can-dogs-eat-onions/) and [garlic](/can-dogs-eat-garlic/) on their own.
